WHAT HAPPENED IN ROUND 1 (the same night)
He asked for a totals protocol. The draft was built on Unders in wind and Unders after a high-scoring first half. The panel reviewed it. Then 990 games of real odds were bought (2023 to November 9 2026): second-half spreads and totals from up to 10 books at two moments of halftime (2 to 7 minutes in, and just before the third-quarter kickoff), and first-half totals just before kickoff. Every claim was fixed in writing before grading. Results, graded at the average book's own number and price, staking to win $300, each game counted once:
- Wind 10 mph or more, Under, second half: 179 games, 52.8 percent won, return minus 0.9 percent. Failed.
- Wind 10 or more, Under, first half: 179 games, 50.9 percent, minus 3.2 percent. Failed.
- First half of 28 or more points, Under, no wind: 209 games, 51.2 percent, minus 4.2 percent. Failed.
- At the best of the posted books instead of the average book, the wind Under returned plus 4.3 percent.
- Books charged about -115 on each side of a second-half total. The average book price was 3.6 points worse than fair.
- Every second-half Under: 48.5 percent won, return minus 9.4 percent. Every Over: 51.5 percent, minus 3.7 percent. By period the Over was 49.5 percent in 2023-2024 and 54.3 percent in 2025-2026 (return plus 1.3 percent).
- Second halves landed 1.1 points above the market's line on average (0.9 in 2023-2024, 1.4 in 2025-2026).
- How the market set the second-half total in 2023-2026: 0.476 x pregame total, plus 0.042 x first-half points, minus 0.065 x halftime lead. Actual second-half points: 0.516 x pregame total, plus 0.030 x first-half points, minus 0.109 x halftime lead.

Then a flow test: ten claims fixed in advance, first-half play-by-play (both teams together) against the same second-half totals.
- Combined first-half yards per play sorts second halves. Over rate against the line by third of yards per play: bottom 45.6 percent, middle 52.6, top 56.1. The same order in both periods (42.5, 51.3, 54.0 and then 49.3, 54.3, 59.1). Top against bottom: 10.5 points, standard error 3.9.

- Under when the first half had more than 23 points on under 4.93 yards per play: 64.9 percent on 57 games. Too few to judge.
- Tempo (number of plays), three-and-out rate, sustained-drive rate and "cheap points" (return scores and short fields) showed nothing steady.
- All the flow numbers together explain 1.0 percent of how far a second half lands from its line.
- The screen was: return above zero in each of two periods with 40 or more games in each, and still above zero one price step worse. A random third of the games passes it 3.9 percent of the time as an Over claim and 0.1 percent as an Under claim.

The engine's premise, each book in turn against the others at halftime:
- A price 3.0 or more points better than the rest appeared in 68 of 901 games, every one a spread: 41-33-1, return plus 5.4 percent. Three in four were gone or smaller five minutes later. Prices 2.0 to 2.9 better went 29-40.
- On totals compared at the same number, no book was 3.0 better in any of 685 games. Books differ on the NUMBER of a total, not on the price at one number: at the first halftime look the books' second-half totals were a full point or more apart in 70 percent of games.
- His engine converts across totals numbers with a flat 1.5 points per half-point, never tested. Measured on real second-half lines of 2011-2021, the share of halves landing exactly on a total is: 17: 11.9 percent, 20: 5.7, 21: 5.0, 23: 4.9, 24: 7.6, 27: 7.2, and 18: 0.0, 22: 2.0, 25: 1.7, 28: 0.9. So a half-point is worth anything from nothing to about 6 points.

Other facts:
- Robinhood: at 45 closing snapshots its all-in half-spread prices averaged 2.4 points worse than fair. None was 2.0 better. Bid-ask about 1 cent. Regulation-only settlement is worth about 0.7 points to a second-half Under and costs an Over about the same.
- His own 46 half bets this season: 12-5 when the price taken was at the closing fair or better, 12-15-2 when worse.
- From his earlier baseball totals protocols: rules added after single bad nights selected against winners, and converting a fair price across numbers with a wrong tie rate (9 percent assumed, 12 to 14 measured) cost money.
